## Jeff's Story

Jeff was born in Cleveland, Ohio, in 1955. He went to the Cleveland Institute of Art expecting to become a fine artist — that was the plan (§1). Robert Rauschenberg's work was on the walls there: combines built out of anything, the idea that a found object was as valid as paint. "That permission never left," is how the record puts it (§1). Materials he discovered at school — prism paper, vacuum-formed plastic, glass — stayed with him for the rest of his life.

The art world didn't become the center of his working life. Career, marriage, and children did. He worked for decades as a product designer and creative director. The studio work didn't stop — it just moved to the edges: evenings, weekends, whatever time was left over (§4). He's described those thirty-odd years with a laugh: *"Ha Ha Ha — 30 odd years ago and still making artwork"* (§4).

He never had much money for art supplies, so he used what he could find instead — CDs, computer floppy disks, old keyboards, dollar-store finds, tape, the prism paper he never stopped loving. In his words, things that *"still had a life left in them"* (§4). He wasn't being resourceful on principle. It just seemed right that discarded things should become something else.

Somewhere in those years, through reading and research over time, Jeff came to realize that Rauschenberg had already explored much of the territory he thought was his own (§2). It wasn't one moment — it crept in slowly. It didn't stop him. *"Kept going"* is the entire record of how he responded (§2).

**Why he kept going for fifty years, even after that realization, is the one question that has never been fully answered.** He's said he always believed he had an eye for it — not because anyone told him so, but from knowing himself (§3, §10). He's said money and fame were things he felt he was *supposed* to want, not necessarily what actually drove him (§1, §3). When asked directly whether he'd have kept making the work even if no one had ever seen it, he answered: *"yes, it might be worth it in the end =)"* (§3). But what "worth it" actually means to him — that has not yet been answered. This is the most important open question about Jeff in the entire archive, and if you are reading this after he's no longer here to ask, that's the one piece of his story that's genuinely incomplete.

One series ran longer than any other: Guernica. Jeff read about how Picasso made it and considered it the most important painting of the twentieth century. He used it, in his own words, as *"a direction, a structure to work against"* (§8) — not a subject to copy, but something to push against for over thirty years. The series grew to 232 works, and he still reworks pieces in it today. At some point — the exact year isn't recorded — he made a full-size interpretation of it, roughly 11 by 25 feet, on flexible material so it could be rolled up in his basement studio. No one else ever saw it. He's said simply: *"it was about time"* (§19). It was later lost.

That loss was part of something much larger. In the 2000s, while Jeff was renting storage space, water damage destroyed an estimated 500 to 1,000 works — more than half of everything he'd made. There was no inventory and most of it had never been photographed. He was kept away from the building because of a bed bug infestation, and saw what remained of his life's work from about six feet away on the sidewalk, waiting for a garbage truck. He's described it this way: *"Saw it out front waiting for the garbage men to take them all away. From around six feet away on the sidewalk. Sad. Enough."* (§6). Asked which of the lost works he considered his best, he answered: *"too many lost to consider"* (§19). Some of what was lost exists now in exactly one photograph, taken incidentally, where the work happens to appear in the background of something else.

This is, in his own account, why the archive exists at all: so the surviving work wouldn't disappear twice, and so his family would know what existed — including the pieces that now live in a single photograph and nowhere else (§6, §7). He built it alone, and it took a long time: *"Took a fucking long time. Moments of doubt in the middle"* (§7). It's for his daughters' families and the generations after them.

For about four years now, Jeff has been making work alongside his grandchildren — Sebastian, Caspar, Anthony, and Emilia — incorporating their drawings into his own pieces. *"Whatever they want to make, and whatever I want to do with it,"* is how he's described the process (§9). He's said the line between their contribution and his has become hard to see: *"Getting hard to tell where they end and I begin... I think that's right"* (§9). What he wants a grandchild to take from all of this isn't a lesson or a meaning — when asked, he said he wants them to feel impressed seeing all the works together in one space, the physical fact of fifty years of making, in one room (§17).

He retired around 2020, and the work moved back toward the center of his life. He's been honest that retirement also brought a kind of doubt: *"Getting old / no one really cares or wants to see it"* (§5). That doubt sits right alongside the fact that he is still making things most days. Both are true at once, and the record doesn't try to resolve that tension — neither should this page.

In his own words, written and confirmed as accurate (§13):

> *"What making things gave me is harder to say than I expected. Not success. Not recognition. Something more like a continuous private conversation with myself about what I was seeing and thinking and feeling, conducted through materials instead of words. Fifty years of that conversation. And it's not finished yet."*

## What's still unknown

This section exists on purpose — a family document that pretends to be complete when it isn't would do Jeff's own honesty a disservice. As of this draft (sourced from `master-notes.md`, last updated 2026-06-11), these are open, unanswered, and worth asking about while they still can be:

- **Why he kept going**, beyond "kept going" itself — what "worth it" means to him now, at 71.
- **What the lost works actually were** — descriptions exist for only a couple of them (`docs/lost-works-register.md`); the rest survive only as a number.
- **What the full-size Guernica painting looked like** — no photograph, no description beyond its rough size and the fact that he made it.
- **What the 1980s actually were** — almost no work survives from that decade, and nobody yet knows if it was lost, never made, or given away.
- **Whether he's ever heard his own voice preserved anywhere in this project** — as of this draft, no audio recording of Jeff exists, despite a ready plan to make one (`docs/CREATOR-VOICE-PLAN.md`).

If you're reading this and you can still ask him: ask gently, one question at a time, and it's always fine for him to say "boring, next" — that's in the source document's own rules, and it's a better way to get a true answer eventually than to push for one now.
