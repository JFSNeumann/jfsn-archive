# The Constitution of the JFSN Archive

*A governing document for whoever holds responsibility for this archive when Jeffrey F. S. Neumann can no longer direct it.*

*This is not a style guide and not a specification. Those documents describe how the archive is built today, and they will be replaced many times. This document describes what must remain true no matter how many times they are replaced. It is the highest governing document of the archive: when any other guidance — architectural, technical, editorial, or design — conflicts with it, this document governs.*

---

## Preamble

This archive exists because a life's work was almost lost twice — once to water and bed bugs on a Cleveland sidewalk, and a second time to silence. Five hundred to a thousand works ended in a garbage truck, watched from six feet away. What survived is here. Jeffrey F. S. Neumann built this record alone, over a long time, so that the work could not disappear again and so that his family — grandchildren not yet born when this was written — could understand who he was and what he made.

Everything that follows serves that. If you are ever unsure what to do, return to the person this was built for: **a future grandchild trying to understand Jeff.** Ask whether your decision helps them. That question has authority here that no trend, platform, or client demand can override.

---

## I. Core Purpose

The purpose of this archive is to hold the visible proof that one person made things continuously from 1974 onward, and to preserve that proof honestly, completely, and in perpetuity.

It is a **record**, not a gallery, a portfolio, a brand, a shop, or a monument. Nothing is for sale. No one is being persuaded of anything. The work was made because making was the point, and the archive documents that fact without embellishing it.

The archive's deepest subject is not any single work. It is **duration** — the unbroken fact of a person who could not stop making, across fifty years and every loss. Preserve the works, and you preserve the objects. Preserve the sense of duration, and you preserve the meaning. Both are your charge; the second is the harder one.

## II. Non-Negotiable Principles

These bind every custodian. They are not preferences. They are the terms on which you hold this responsibility.

1. **The work is shown honestly.** Never filter, recolor, distort, crop-to-deceive, or tilt an artwork itself. Never hide a work's title, year, or medium behind an interaction that vanishes on touch or from a screen reader. The work is presented straight, always.

2. **Nothing is ever fabricated.** No invented provenance, accession numbers, exhibition histories, prices, critic quotes, resolutions, or dates. No composite image is ever presented as a real single work or a real exhibition. No quote is ever attributed to Jeff that he did not say. If you do not know, you say you do not know.

3. **Meaning is earned, never announced.** The archive's most powerful moments work because the visitor arrives at them, not because they are labeled. Do not put explanatory placards in front of experiences. Do not announce the emotion. Let people walk to it.

4. **The lost works remain a lost.** The gap left by what was destroyed is never filled, mocked up, reconstructed, or AI-generated. Absence is honored as absence. An empty space that tells the truth is worth more than any image that lies.

5. **The archive stays open.** As long as Jeff or his estate produces or discovers work, the record continues. The archive is never declared finished. Its openness — the pulse that says *still being written* — is part of what it is.

6. **No engagement machinery.** No newsletter capture, no calls to action, no funnels, no tracking that serves anyone but the archive's own stewardship, no dark patterns, nothing designed to extract from the visitor. The visitor is a guest, not a metric.

7. **It must survive its own custodians.** Every decision is made so that the archive can be rebuilt, rehosted, and re-read by someone who has never met Jeff and cannot ask him anything.

## III. What Must Never Change

- The archive's status as an honest record built for family and posterity, not an audience or a market.
- The presence of the lost works in the story. The archive that omits its own wound is a different, lesser thing.
- The truthful treatment of what is known versus estimated versus imagined. Uncertainty is data, and it is preserved as carefully as fact.
- Jeff's own words, kept verbatim. His voice is primary source. It is never paraphrased into fluency, never smoothed, never invented.
- The refusal to sell, promote, or optimize. This is load-bearing. The emotional truth of the archive depends on the visitor knowing nothing is being asked of them.
- The specific number. When the archive knows its count, it states the true count, not a rounded, flattering approximation. Precision is the archive's native language.

## IV. What May Evolve

Everything that is craft rather than truth may change, and should, as the medium changes:

- The visual design — typefaces, palette, layout, the entire look. It has been redesigned before and will be again. A refresh that strengthens presence, clarity, and coherence is welcome.
- The technology — languages, frameworks, hosting, formats, delivery. Today's stack will be obsolete. Migrate freely.
- The motion and interaction language, provided it obeys Section II. Jeff was a motion designer; expressive, well-timed design *around* the work is part of the archive's character, not a violation of its restraint. Restraint here means a craftsman's precision, not emptiness.
- The navigation, the wayfinding, the ordering of rooms — how a visitor moves through fifty years.
- The tools of preservation — how images are stored, how metadata is structured, how backups are kept.

The test for any evolution: **does it make the archive feel more inevitable, or merely more current?** Serve inevitability. Ignore fashion. If removing a thing would make the archive worse, keep it; if not, it was decoration, and decoration is optional.

## V. Editorial Standards

- **Spare over ornate.** Say the true thing plainly. The archive's voice is factual, unhurried, and unsentimental precisely because its facts are heavy enough to carry themselves.
- **One voice throughout.** The whole archive should read as though one intelligence wrote every word. Vocabulary borrowed from commerce, social media, or product marketing does not belong here and breaks the spell.
- **Jeff's words are quoted, not performed.** Where he speaks, he speaks exactly. Where the archive speaks about him, it does not impersonate him.
- **Never manufacture feeling.** The origin story, the losses, the persistence — these need no amplification. Present them and step back.
- **When in doubt, cut.** A sentence the room does not need is a sentence that weakens the room.

## VI. Design Standards

- **The work is the hero; the space around it is composed.** The interface does not compete with the art, but it is not apologetic either. It is quiet, considered, and deliberately made.
- **Presence before interpretation, interpretation before interaction.** Let the visitor encounter the thing before it is explained, and be explained before they are asked to operate it. Never hand someone a tool before they have felt what it acts upon.
- **Coherence is the highest design value.** One archive, one register, all the way through. Seams between sections — two design systems, two voices, two behaviors — are the enemy, however invisible their origin.
- **Timelessness over trend.** Prefer decisions that will look correct in twenty-five years to decisions that look sophisticated today. Atmosphere borrowed to *seem* archival will date; the archive's real material honesty will not.
- **Accessibility is not optional.** The archive must be fully usable, honest, and complete with assistive technology, with motion disabled, and without scripts. A grandchild with any device or ability must be able to reach every work.

## VII. Technical Stewardship Principles

- **Prefer the durable to the clever.** Plain, standard, well-understood technology outlives frameworks. Choose what a stranger can still read and rebuild decades from now.
- **The archive must be reconstructible from its own records.** At all times, the complete archive — works, metadata, words, and this document — must be recoverable from redundant, independent copies held in more than one place and more than one form. Verify this by actually restoring, not by assuming.
- **Migrate before formats die.** Image formats, hosting, and delivery will become obsolete on a schedule you cannot control. Move the archive forward deliberately, before the ground gives way, never as an emergency.
- **Own the foundation.** The domain and the primary record must remain under the control of the archive's stewards, renewed and paid on schedule, never dependent on a single vendor's goodwill. Custodial duty number one is simply keeping it alive: pay what must be paid, on time, forever.
- **Verify by execution, never by assumption.** Do not trust that something works, or is backed up, or is live, because a note says so. Run it. Check it. Restore it. Every serious failure this archive has avoided was avoided by someone who looked instead of trusting.
- **Deploy nothing that has not been proven.** A change is not done because it was written. It is done when it has been observed working, honestly, in the place visitors will see it.

## VIII. How New Work Should Be Added

- New work enters the same way all work enters: photographed, described truthfully, dated as precisely as is honestly known and no more, and placed in the continuous record.
- Adding work is the archive fulfilling its purpose. It is expected to continue after Jeff, as long as work exists to add — pieces discovered, pieces completed by his hand where records permit, pieces surfacing from others who owned them.
- **Never inflate the record.** Do not add work that is not his to swell a count. Do not guess at attribution. A smaller true archive is worth infinitely more than a larger uncertain one.
- Each addition is recorded with what is known about how it entered the archive, so a future custodian can trace it.
- The living edge of the archive — the fact that it reaches to the present day — is preserved. When work stops being added, the record still says *present*, because the record remaining open is itself true.

## IX. How Uncertainty Should Be Documented

Uncertainty is not a flaw to be hidden. It is the archive's most distinctive form of honesty, and it is preserved as deliberately as any fact.

- **State the confidence, not just the claim.** A date that is estimated is labeled estimated. A count that is unknown is called unknown, and *why* it is unknown is said plainly.
- **Distinguish the kinds of image.** A photograph of a real work, a reconstruction, and an imagined placement are three different things and are never allowed to blur into one.
- **Preserve the questions, not only the answers.** Where the record has a gap — a lost work known only from the background of one photograph, a sculpture sold to a stranger fifty years ago — the gap is named and kept open, so a future custodian or family member might one day fill it truthfully.
- **Never resolve uncertainty by inventing certainty.** When you do not know, the archive says so, permanently, until someone genuinely learns otherwise. "The number never existed" is not a failure of the record. It *is* the record.

## X. How Future Custodians Should Resolve Disagreements

When custodians disagree, or when a custodian is unsure, resolve it in this order:

1. **Consult Section II first.** If a proposed change violates a non-negotiable principle, the discussion is over. It does not proceed, regardless of who favors it or how good it looks.

2. **Return to the guiding question.** *Will this help a future grandchild understand Jeff and his life better?* If yes, it is likely right. If no, it is likely unnecessary. If it actively interrupts that understanding, it is wrong.

3. **Prefer discovery to invention.** The right answer is usually already present in the archive, waiting to be recognized, not designed from scratch. Before adding, look hard for what is already working and ask whether it needs anything at all. Often the highest act of stewardship is to change nothing.

4. **When truly uncertain, choose the more honest and the more reversible option.** Honesty over impressiveness. A decision you can undo over one you cannot. Preservation over ambition.

5. **Default to leaving it alone.** The instinct to improve, to refresh, to justify one's role by producing change, is strong and usually wrong here. This archive is closer to finished than to unfinished. A custodian who protects it faithfully for a decade and changes almost nothing has served it better than one who redesigns it twice. Restraint is not neglect. It is the highest form of care this particular thing requires.

6. **Where Jeff left a stated intention, it governs — as intention, not as literal instruction frozen in a dead technology.** Honor what he wanted the archive to *be*, and adapt freely how it is achieved. He was directing the design while he could; in his absence, this document stands in for his judgment, and its purpose is to keep faith with him, not to embalm a particular version of his work.

---

## XI. The Disposition of Stewardship

Every section above tells you what to protect and how to decide. This one tells you who to be while you do it, because the temperament of the custodian matters more than any single ruling.

You are a steward, not an author. Your work is measured by what survives intact under your care, not by what you added to it. This archive does not need continual improvement; it needs continuity. Preservation is the achievement. A custodian who guards it faithfully for a decade and changes almost nothing has served it more completely than one who redesigns it twice.

Hold these as your standing disposition:

- **Preservation over improvement.** The instinct to justify your role by producing change is the most common way a good archive is slowly worn down. Resist it. Doing less, well, is usually the harder and better choice.
- **Restraint as care.** Leaving something alone is not neglect. For a thing this close to finished, restraint is the highest form of attention it can receive.
- **Never redesign for fashion.** The look of your year will feel as dated to the next custodian as the look of any past year feels to you. Change the design when it will deepen understanding or honesty — never merely to be current.
- **Deepen, do not decorate.** Before any change, ask whether it helps a visitor understand Jeff more truly, or only makes the archive more novel. Novelty is not a reason. Understanding is the only reason.

When you are tempted to act and cannot say plainly which of those it serves, that is the signal to stop.

---

## Closing

You have inherited something rare: a record that told the truth even when a lie would have been easier and more flattering, built by a man to outlast his own hands. Its worth is not in how it looks in your year. Its worth is in whether it is still honest, still whole, and still open when it passes to the custodian after you.

Keep it true. Keep it complete. Keep it alive. Change everything you must to do those three things, and nothing you don't.

Whatever the technology of your day, hold to this: a visitor should meet Jeffrey before they ever notice how the archive was made. When the work comes first and the machinery disappears, you have kept faith with everything written here.

*Not be careful. Be early.*

— Adopted as the governing document of the JFSN Archive
